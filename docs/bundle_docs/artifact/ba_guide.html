Part I

The problem

What is being minimised, and why that particular quantity — before any algorithm appears. If you can already write down the reprojection objective and its parameter blocks from memory, start at Part II.

§1 Starting from zero

Nothing in this section is specific to photogrammetry. It exists so that every term used later — residual, least squares, linearisation, normal equations, linear solver — has been defined before it is relied on. If these are already reflexes, skip to §2.

What is actually being asked

You have a few hundred photographs. Somewhere in the world there is a rock, and it appears as a smudge of pixels at coordinate (1043.2, 88.7) in image 12, and at (77.9, 512.4) in image 13, and so on. You have a guess at where each camera was, and a guess at where the rock is. Those guesses are wrong, and you can tell they are wrong because if you take your guessed rock, and mathematically photograph it with your guessed camera, the pixel you predict is not the pixel you measured.

That mismatch — predicted minus measured, in pixels — is a residual. Bundle adjustment is the act of nudging every camera and every point simultaneously until the residuals are collectively as small as possible. “Bundle” refers to the bundle of light rays converging on each camera centre; “adjustment” is the surveyor’s word for fitting observations to a model.

Why squares?

Given many residuals, you need a single number that says how bad the current guess is, so that “better” is well defined. Summing residuals directly fails (positives cancel negatives). Summing absolute values works but is not differentiable at zero. Summing squares is differentiable everywhere, penalises large errors disproportionately, and — the real reason — is the maximum-likelihood estimate when measurement errors are independent and Gaussian. So the objective is

C(x)  =  12 ‖f(x)‖²  =  12 ∑i fi(x)²

where x collects every unknown (all camera poses, all 3D points, all intrinsics) into one long vector, and f stacks every residual into one long vector. The ½ is bookkeeping: it cancels the 2 that appears on differentiating.

Linear least squares: solved once, in closed form

If the residual happens to be linear in the unknowns — f(x) = Ax − b — then C is an exact quadratic bowl. It has one minimum, and calculus finds it in a single step by setting the derivative to zero:

A⊤A x  =  A⊤b

These are the normal equations. One solve, no iteration, no initial guess. This is the case treated in Nocedal & Wright §10.2.

Bundle adjustment is not that case

Projecting a 3D point into an image requires rotating it (trigonometric in the rotation parameters), then dividing by depth (a perspective division, u = f·X/Z), then optionally applying lens distortion (polynomial in radius). None of those are linear. So C is not a bowl; it is a landscape, and there is no formula for its lowest point.

The universal escape is: replace the hard nonlinear problem with an easy linear one that is accurate nearby, solve that, move, and repeat. Ceres states the strategy directly:

Ceres Solver · nnls_solving.rstsource
"The general strategy when solving non-linear optimization problems is to solve a sequence of approximations to the original problem. At each iteration, the approximation is solved to determine a correction Δx to the vector x. For non-linear least squares, an approximation can be constructed by using the linearization F(x + Δx) ≈ F(x) + J(x)Δx which leads to the following linear least squares problem: minΔx ½ ||J(x)Δx + F(x)||²"

That single quoted sentence generates the entire rest of this document. Each iteration needs (a) the linearisation — the matrix J, which is §3; (b) a rule for how far to trust it, which is §5; and (c) a way to actually solve the resulting linear system, which is §6–§8.

And immediately, the catch, from the same source: “Unfortunately, naively solving a sequence of these problems and updating x ← x + Δx leads to an algorithm that may not converge. To get a convergent algorithm, we need to control the size of the step Δx.” Everything in §5 exists to control that step size.

§2 The problem being solved

Bundle adjustment minimises

C(x)  =  12 ∑i  ρi ( ‖fi(x)‖² )

This is Ceres’s own statement of its problem class — “arg minx ½‖F(x)‖²” — with ρi the loss attached to block i (§10).

The gauge

A free network of cameras and points is determined only up to a 7-parameter similarity (3 rotation, 3 translation, 1 scale): applying it to every camera and point at once leaves every reprojection unchanged, so ∇²C carries a null space of dimension ≥7 and the normal equations are rank-deficient by construction — Triggs et al. (2000) devote §9 to exactly this. COLMAP’s TWO_CAMS_FROM_WORLD gauge fixes it directly: the first frame’s full pose is held constant (6 DoF) and one translation component of a second frame is frozen (1 DoF) — 7 in total, at bundle_adjustment_ceres.cc:397–416. This repo selects it automatically whenever nothing else anchors the block (bundle.py:550–555); when markers, pose priors, or a reference cloud are present, those residuals supply the datum instead.

Part II

One iteration of the outer solver

Every method in this family does the same three things over and over: build a linear model of the residuals, decide how far that model can be trusted, take a step. These three sections are that loop — and nothing in them is specific to photogrammetry.

§3 The Jacobian, and what “inverting the design matrix” means

Stack every residual into f(x)∈ℝm. The Jacobian — photogrammetry’s design matrix A — is J = ∂f/∂x ∈ ℝm×n. Ceres never assembles it densely: each residual block writes a small dense strip (for a tie point, 2×3 for the point, 2×6 for the pose tangent, 2×k for intrinsics) into a block-sparse structure.

Analytic, not autodiff, for the residual you actually run

COLMAP ships two implementations of the reprojection cost: an analytic SizedCostFunction<2,3,7,N> with hand-derived Jacobians (chain rule J_point = J_uvw · R, reprojection_error.h:126–133), and an autodiff functor via ceres::AutoDiffCostFunction. Dispatch is compile-time, based on whether a camera model declares has_img_from_cam_with_jac. Checking every model declaration in sensor/models.h:469–789 of this COLMAP build: all of them declare it, so a plain COLMAP bundle uses the analytic Jacobian; autodiff remains the path for rig costs and pose priors.

verifiedv2_jac.py · analytic Jacobian vs. central finite differences, SIMPLE_RADIAL
J shape: (2, 14) 2 residuals × [3 point + 7 pose + 4 intrinsics] max |J_ceres − J_finite_difference| = 4.12e-08 relative error = 2.05e-11

The structure checks out entry by entry: columns 11–12 give ∂(u,v)/∂(cx,cy) = I exactly (principal point shifts the projection 1:1); the translation column gives ∂u/∂tx ≈ 99.6 ≈ f/Z, the textbook pinhole derivative.

The normal equations — and why nobody inverts J

Linearise f(x+Δ) ≈ f + JΔ and substitute into C = ½‖f‖²:

m(Δ)  =  ½f⊤f  +  Δ⊤J⊤f  +  ½Δ⊤J⊤JΔ

Setting ∇m = 0:

J⊤J Δx  =  −J⊤f    i.e.    HΔx = −g

the Gauss–Newton step, with g = J⊤f = ∇C the exact gradient (Nocedal & Wright, Numerical Optimization, 2nd ed., §10.1, eq. 10.4). J is m×n and rectangular — it has no inverse. “Inverting the design matrix” means, computationally: (1) form H = J⊤J, g = J⊤f, possibly only implicitly; (2) factorise — Cholesky H = LL⊤, or QR of J directly — and solve two triangular systems; (3) only later, and only for selected blocks, compute entries of H−1 for covariance. Step 3 is the only place an actual inverse appears, and even then never densely for a real problem.

Three separate reasons, worth keeping separate

  1. J has no inverse. It is m×n with m ≫ n (far more residual rows than parameter columns). “Inverting J” is not a defined operation; the phrase is always shorthand for solving the least-squares system.
  2. Forming an explicit inverse is both expensive and self-defeating. Inverting an n×n matrix is O(n³) and, worse, the inverse of a sparse matrix is dense. In BA n = 6·(cameras) + 3·(points), routinely 106–108. Densifying it destroys exactly the structure §7 and §8 exist to exploit.
  3. Forming H = J⊤J costs accuracy. This one is subtle and worth the algebra.

Write the SVD J = UΣV⊤. Then J⊤J = VΣ⊤U⊤UΣV⊤ = VΣ²V⊤. The singular values of J are squared in J⊤J, so the condition number — their ratio — is squared too:

κ(J⊤J)  =  κ(J)²

A Jacobian with a merely awkward κ(J) = 10⁵ yields normal equations at 1010, and double precision carries about 16 digits. Triggs et al. quantify the consequence directly — footnote 6 of §4.3:

Triggs et al. 2000, §4.3 fn. 6source
"The QR method gives the solution to a relative error of about O(Cε), as compared to O(C²ε) for the normal equations, where C is the condition number (the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value) of J, and ε is machine precision."

So working with J directly via QR is a genuine accuracy win, at (per Triggs) “up to a factor of two slower”. Ceres exposes both routes — DENSE_QR and DENSE_NORMAL_CHOLESKY, the latter described in its docs as “faster but numerically less stable than QR”. For BA the normal-equations route wins anyway, because only it exposes the block structure of §7; the damping of §5 is what keeps the conditioning survivable.

A detail that matters: Ceres does not add damping to a formed J⊤J. It appends rows to the Jacobian — “the matrix (1/√μ)D has been concatenated at the bottom of the matrix J(x) and a corresponding vector of zeroes has been added to the bottom of F(x)”. Algebraically identical, numerically better, for exactly the squaring reason above.

§4 Gauss–Newton, and what it throws away

Differentiate C(x) = ½f(x)⊤f(x) twice:

∇²C  =  J⊤J  +  ∑i fi∇²fi

(Nocedal & Wright, 2nd ed., eqs. 10.4–10.5.) Gauss–Newton keeps only J⊤J and drops the curvature term — a sum of residuals weighted by the curvature of the residual functions. That’s a good approximation exactly when residuals are small at the solution, or the residual functions are nearly affine. BA near convergence is the first case: sub-pixel residuals against J⊤J entries of order (f/Z)² ~ 10⁴.

The neglected term is theoretically O(‖f‖). Measured: RMS residual grows 0.391→6.265 px (×16.0) and the ratio grows 1.47e-4→2.35e-3 (×16.0) — the predicted linear scaling, to two significant figures.

It fails when residuals stay large at the optimum (a mis-modelled camera, gross outliers — Nocedal & Wright §10.3 treat this directly), and whenever J⊤J is singular: an unfixed gauge (§12), or an unobservable parameter such as focal length on a flat nadir block. Both are cured by the same device — damping.

§5 Trust region, line search, and Levenberg–Marquardt

Line search picks a direction first, then a step length along it (steepest descent, BFGS, Wolfe conditions) — cheap, gradient-only, and a poor fit for BA. Trust region picks a step-length bound first, then solves the best-fit step inside it. This is Ceres’s default, and therefore COLMAP’s, and therefore this repo’s:

Ceres trust-region loop — docs/source/nnls_solving.rstsource
1. Given x and trust-region radius μ. 2. Solve argmin_dx ½||J(x)dx + F(x)||² s.t. ||D(x)dx||² ≤ μ 3. ρ = (||F(x+dx)||² − ||F(x)||²) / (||J(x)dx+F(x)||² − ||F(x)||²) 4. if ρ > eps then x = x + dx 5. if ρ > eta_1 then μ = 2μ 6. else if ρ < eta_2 then μ = 0.5μ 7. go to 2.

ρ is actual decrease over predicted decrease — it tells the algorithm whether the quadratic model deserved to be trusted at that step length, and the radius adapts accordingly.

LM as a trust-region method

The Lagrangian of the constrained subproblem, stationary in Δ, gives

( J⊤J  +  λ′ D⊤D )Δ  =  −J⊤f

Ceres writes the unconstrained equivalent as argminΔx ½‖JΔx+F‖² + (1/μ)‖DΔx‖², so the damping is 2/μ, and D is “typically the square root of the diagonal of J⊤J” (Marquardt scaling — unit-invariant across parameters). The two limits:

  • μ → ∞ (large trust region): damping vanishes, Δ → the pure Gauss–Newton step.
  • μ → 0 (tiny trust region): Δ → −(μ/2)(D⊤D)−1J⊤f, an infinitesimal, diagonally-scaled steepest-descent step.

J⊤J + λD⊤D is positive definite for any λ>0, even when J⊤J itself is singular — this is precisely what lets a gauge-free network be solved at all.

Dogleg

Ceres’s alternative precomputes two μ-independent vectors — the Gauss–Newton step and the Cauchy (steepest-descent) point — and interpolates along the path between them (Powell’s TRADITIONAL_DOGLEG) or over their full 2-D span (Byrd–Schnabel SUBSPACE_DOGLEG). Advantage, from the Ceres docs: it “only solves one linear system per successful step” where LM “may need to solve an arbitrary number.” Disadvantage, same source: it “can only be used with exact factorization based linear solvers” — it needs a well-defined GN step, so it’s unusable with ITERATIVE_SCHUR and fragile on a rank-deficient, gauge-free H. LM is the safer default for BA, and what COLMAP leaves in place.

Part III

The inner solve, where BA stops being generic

Every step attempt in Part II ends in a linear system with up to millions of unknowns, and it is attempted many times per solve. That this is tractable at all is due entirely to structure — the subject of these three sections, and the one place where bundle adjustment genuinely differs from generic nonlinear least squares.

§6 What “the linear solver” is

At every trust-region step attempt — accepted or rejected — something has to solve (H+λD⊤D)Δx = −g. “Linear solver type” is Ceres’s name for how.

The governing quantity is camera count, never point count — which only makes sense once you’ve seen why the Schur complement collapses one and not the other.

§7 The sparse structure and the Schur complement

A tie-point residual touches exactly one point, one pose, one camera — nothing else. So H = J⊤J is bipartite: point–point entries are non-zero only for the same point, giving a block-diagonal C. Verified on a toy 5-camera / 40-point block (v3_schur.py): C is exactly block diagonal: max |off-block| = 0.000e+00.

Derivation

From the second block row of Hz = g, E⊤Δy + CΔz = w, and since C is block-diagonal with invertible 3×3 blocks:

Δz  =  C−1( w − E⊤Δy )

Substituting into the first row, BΔy + EΔz = v:

[ B − EC−1E⊤ ] Δy  =  v − EC−1w

S = B−EC−1E⊤ is the Schur complement of C in H — the reduced camera matrix, credited by Ceres to Brown (1958); it is photogrammetry’s own reduced normal equations, decades older than computer vision’s name for it. This is exact block Gaussian elimination, not an approximation — it factors as H = L·diag(S,C)·L⊤ with L unit triangular, invertible by construction, requiring only that each 3×3 block of C be invertible (guaranteed by damping).

What “simplifying a sparse problem” means, concretely

(a) Fill-reducing reordering on S. S is itself sparse: block Sij is non-zero iff cameras i and j share an observed point — S is the co-visibility graph, in matrix form. Verified on a 10-camera flight strip with genuine limited overlap (v9b.py): the block-sparsity pattern of S matches the co-visibility matrix exactly, and shows the expected band structure of a strip. Sparse Cholesky with a fill-reducing ordering (AMD — Amestoy, Davis & Duff, 2004) exploits precisely this; a naive ordering would fill in the band.

(b) Never form S at all. CG only needs matrix–vector products Sx, computable from B, E, C−1 without ever assembling S:

verifiedv7_cg.py · matrix-free Schur + Jacobi-PCG vs. direct solve, 8 cams / 150 pts

This is what ITERATIVE_SCHUR does. (c) Preconditioning: SCHUR_JACOBI uses S’s cheap-to-compute block diagonal; the justification κ(S) ≤ κ(H) is due to Agarwal, Snavely, Seitz & Szeliski (2010) — CG on the reduced system is better conditioned than CG on H directly.

Where this repo sits: ≤50 images → DENSE_SCHUR; a few hundred (a typical aerial block) → SPARSE_SCHUR; only above 1000 does ITERATIVE_SCHUR engage.

§8 Fill-in, ordering, and preconditioning

§7 asserted three things without justifying them: that a bad elimination order is catastrophic, that the Schur order is a choice rather than a law, and that preconditioning is what makes the iterative path work. This section makes good on all three.

What fill-in actually is

Cholesky factorises H = LL⊤. The catch is that L is generally denser than H. When you eliminate a variable, every pair of variables it was connected to becomes connected to each other — a new non-zero that was not in the original matrix. That is fill-in, and it is a statement about a graph, not about numbers: draw a node per parameter block and an edge wherever a residual couples two blocks, and eliminating a node means removing it and adding a clique among its former neighbours.

This is why the order matters so much, and Ceres does not understate it:

Ceres Solver · nnls_solving.rst, §Orderingsource
"The order in which variables are eliminated in a linear solver can have a significant of impact on the efficiency and accuracy of the method. For example when doing sparse Cholesky factorization, there are matrices for which a good ordering will give a Cholesky factor with O(n) storage, whereas a bad ordering will result in an completely dense factor."

Dellaert & Kaess give a measured instance of the same effect on a simulated SLAM problem: switching from a naive landmarks-first order to a COLAMD ordering drops the number of non-zeros in R from approximately 2.8 million to about 250 thousand — a factor of 11, for free, from reordering alone. They also note the bad news: finding the optimal ordering is NP-complete, so every practical system uses a heuristic.

The Schur order is a heuristic too — and it has a cost

It is tempting to read §7 as “eliminate points first, always”. The honest version is more interesting. Eliminating points first is cheap per se (C is block-diagonal, so C−1 is q independent 3×3 inversions), but by the clique rule above it makes every pair of cameras that shared a point into neighbours. Dellaert & Kaess put it plainly:

Dellaert & Kaess, Factor Graphs for Robot Perception, §4.5source
"In SLAM, a naive ordering strategy is to eliminate the landmarks first, and then the poses. This is often called the 'Schur-complement trick', because when the elimination is written down at the block level the corresponding linear algebra operation involves taking the Schur complement of a matrix." "However, 'natural' orderings such as the Schur complement trick ... can be quite expensive as compared to using better heuristics. In structure from motion, with possibly hundreds of thousands of points, it can be advantageous to use the Schur complement trick as it is beneficial in terms of cache coherence and locality." "In Figure 4.4 the landmarks were eliminated first, leading to a densely connected graph on the robot poses. Doing the reverse (poses first) would lead to a fully connected clique of landmarks."

So S is denser than B was — the Schur complement creates fill-in in the camera block. It is still overwhelmingly worth it in SfM because q ≫ p, and because the resulting inner loop is cache-friendly. But it is a domain-specific heuristic that happens to be excellent here, not a theorem. The correct mental model is two orderings, applied in sequence: a block-level one (points first — Schur), then a fill-reducing scalar one on the reduced system S.

Who chooses, and how

Ceres formalises the block-level choice as elimination groups, with one non-negotiable requirement:

Ceres Solver · nnls_solving.rst, §Ordering → Bundle Adjustmentsource
"The lowest numbered elimination group must form an independent set in the graph corresponding to the Hessian, or in other words, no two parameter blocks in the first elimination group should co-occur in the same residual block. For the best performance, this elimination group should be as large as possible. For standard bundle adjustment problems, this corresponds to the first elimination group containing all the 3d points, and the second containing the parameter blocks for all the cameras. If the user leaves the choice to Ceres, then the solver uses an approximate maximum independent set algorithm to identify the first group."

“Independent set” is precisely the condition that makes C block-diagonal — no two points ever appear in the same residual. The requirement and the structure are the same fact stated twice.

Within a group, the scalar ordering is delegated to a standard algorithm:

Ceres’s own summary of SPARSE_SCHUR: it uses “row and column re-ordering algorithms to maximize the sparsity of the Cholesky decomposition, and focus their compute effort on the non-zero part of the factorization”, which “allow bundle adjustment algorithms to scenes with thousands of cameras.”

The iterative branch: preconditioning is the whole game

Part IV

What you feed the solver

Parts II and III are indifferent to what is being fitted; swap the residual and they run unchanged. These four sections are the modelling decisions that decide what the solver actually converges to — the residual, the robustifier, the parameterisation of rotation, and the choice of datum. Getting the algorithm right and these wrong produces a fast, confident, incorrect answer.

§9 Cost functions: what actually varies

A cost function is a residual block plus its Jacobians. What differs between formulations:

Pixel space, not normalised

COLMAP’s residual is pixels through the real camera model (§2), so the distortion coefficients sit inside the residual and are jointly refinable, and the residual is already in the units the measurement noise is specified in — an unweighted ‖f‖² is an approximately correct ML objective under isotropic Gaussian pixel noise. Normalised-coordinate residuals (common in SLAM) are cheaper but cannot refine distortion; COLMAP does not use them.

Weighted vs. unweighted — Mahalanobis whitening

Given measurement covariance Σ, the correct ML objective is ½f⊤Σ−1f. With Σ−1=LL⊤ (Cholesky), whitening the residual itself, r=L⊤f, gives ‖r‖²=f⊤Σ−1f — a plain sum of squares Ceres can consume directly. COLMAP’s CovarianceWeightedCostFunctor implements exactly this (cost_functions/utils.h:124–165, LeftSqrtInformation = cov.inverse().llt().matrixL().transpose()).

§10 Loss functions: robustification

With a loss, a block contributes ½ρ(s), s=‖f‖². Differentiating:

∂/∂x  [½ρ(s)]  =  ρ′(s) J⊤f

Dropping the resulting rank-one second-order term gives the IRLS form — every block reweighted by wi=ρ′(si), recomputed each iteration:

  (∑i ρ′(si) Ji⊤Ji)Δx  =  −∑i ρ′(si) Ji⊤fi

Ceres implements this by rescaling the residual and Jacobian directly (ceres::Corrector: r←√ρ′·r), so the rest of the solver never knows a loss was present. Triggs et al. (2000) §4.3 give the full treatment including the rank-one correction needed when ρ″>0.

Practical reading

  • Trivial — correct only if residuals really are Gaussian; one 100px outlier contributes 10⁴ to the cost.
  • Huber — quadratic inside a, linear outside; convex, so no spurious minima; weight decays only as 1/|r|, so a far outlier still pulls, just boundedly.
  • Soft-L1 — a C∞ version of Huber, same asymptotics, no kink in ρ″ to upset the trust-region model. COLMAP’s local-BA default, scale 1.0.

A non-trivial loss makes the sum of squares no longer χ²-distributed, so a variance factor computed from raw residuals is biased — the standard remedy replaces the block count with the effective redundancy Σρ′(si) when estimating σ̂₀².

§11 Manifolds: why a rotation is not four numbers

Everything so far assumed x is a plain vector you can add a step to. For rotations that is false, and pretending otherwise produces a singular system on every iteration.

The problem, in one paragraph

A rotation in 3D has three degrees of freedom. A unit quaternion representing it has four numbers, tied by the constraint ‖q‖ = 1. Suppose you ignore that and let the optimiser move q freely in ℝ⁴. Scaling q by any λ > 0 gives the same rotation — the rotation matrix built from q is homogeneous of degree zero in q. So the cost is exactly flat along the radial direction q itself:

C(λq)  =  C(q)  ⇒  ∇C · q  =  0  ⇒  J⊤J q  =  0

which is to say every unconstrained quaternion contributes its own null direction to the normal equations. A hundred cameras means a hundred structural singularities. LM’s damping will paper over it and the solve will limp along, but the step is meaningless in that direction and any covariance you extract is nonsense. The fix is not to normalise after each step — that is a patch on the symptom. The fix is to stop taking steps in ℝ⁴ at all.

Ambient space, tangent space, retraction

Ceres Solver · nnls_modeling.rst, §Manifoldsource
"Manifolds are spaces which locally look like Euclidean spaces. More precisely, at each point on the manifold there is a linear space that is tangent to the manifold. It has dimension equal to the intrinsic dimension of the manifold itself, which is less than or equal to the ambient space in which the manifold is embedded." "There are two reasons tangent spaces are interesting: 1. They are Euclidean spaces so the usual vector space operations apply there ... 2. Movements in the tangent space translate into movements along the manifold. Movements perpendicular to the tangent space do not translate into movements on the manifold."

So the parameter block keeps its ambient size (4 numbers for a quaternion, stored as-is), but the optimiser searches in a tangent space of the true dimension (3). The bridge between them is the ⊞ operator, a retraction:

Ceres Solver · nnls_modeling.rst, §Manifoldsource
"A basic operation one can perform on a manifold is the ⊞ operation that computes the result of moving along δ in the tangent space at x, and then projecting back onto the manifold that x belongs to. Also known as a Retraction, ⊞ is a generalization of vector addition in Euclidean spaces." "For SO(3), ⊞ and ⊟ are defined in terms of the matrix exp and log operations: ⊞(x, Δ) = x·exp(Δ) ⊟(y, x) = log(x⊤y)"

The update rule of §5 therefore becomes x ← x ⊞ Δ, and the chain rule picks up one extra factor. If D(x) = ∂⊞(x,Δ)/∂Δ at Δ = 0 is the manifold’s PlusJacobian (an ambient×tangent matrix), then the Jacobian Ceres assembles is

Jtangent  =  Jambient · D(x)

and every system in Parts II–III — H, g, S, the covariance blocks — is expressed in tangent coordinates. A 7-parameter pose block contributes 6 columns, not 7. This is worth stating loudly because it is the single most common source of confusion when indexing into a covariance matrix by hand.

Ceres gives the payoff explicitly: a manifold “naturally constrains the quantity to the manifold throughout the optimization, freeing the user from hacks like quaternion normalization”, and “reduces the dimension of the optimization problem to its natural size … not just the computational complexity … but also improves the numerical behaviour of the algorithm.”

The consistency axioms

Not every projection-back-onto-the-manifold qualifies. Ceres requires four properties, which are worth reading as a definition of “a sane local coordinate system”:

Ceres Solver · nnls_modeling.rst, §Manifoldsource
"1. ⊞(x, 0) = x. This ensures that the tangent space is centered at x, and the zero vector is the identity element. 2. For all y on the manifold, ⊞(x, ⊟(y,x)) = y. This ensures that any y can be reached from x. 3. For all Δ, ⊟(⊞(x, Δ), x) = Δ. This ensures that ⊞ is an injective map. 4. For all Δ₁, Δ₂: |⊟(⊞(x,Δ₁), ⊞(x,Δ₂))| ≤ |Δ₁ − Δ₂|. Allows us to define a metric on the manifold."

The framework is due to Hertzberg, Wagner, Frese & Schröder, “Integrating Generic Sensor Fusion Algorithms with Sound State Representations through Encapsulation of Manifolds”, Information Fusion 14(1), 2013 — the paper that made ⊞/⊟ standard vocabulary in robotics and SfM alike.

§12 Gauge freedom: the seven directions the data cannot see

§11 was about a singularity you create by bad parameterisation. This one you cannot parameterise away: it is a property of the problem, and it is the reason a “converged” bundle can still have an infinitely uncertain answer.

The argument

Take a converged reconstruction. Rotate every camera and every point about the origin by the same rotation. Every point’s position in every camera frame is unchanged, so every projection is unchanged, so every residual is unchanged, so the cost is unchanged. The same holds for translating the whole scene, and — because projection is a homogeneous operation, u = f·X/Z — for scaling the whole scene. Three rotations, three translations, one scale:

ng  =  3  +  3  +  1  =  7

These are the gauge freedoms, and Triggs et al. devote §9 to them. The consequence for the linear algebra is exact, not approximate:

Triggs et al. 2000, §9.2source
"Let G be any n_x × n_g matrix whose columns span the local gauge orbit directions at x. By the exact gauge invariance of f, its gradient and Hessian vanish along orbit directions: g⊤G = 0 and HG = 0. Note that the gauged Hessian H is singular with (at least) rank deficiency n_g and null space G. This is called gauge deficiency. Many numerical optimization routines assume nonsingular H, and must be modified to work in gauge invariant problems." "The singularity is an expression of indifference: any two updates ending on the same gauge orbit are equivalent, both in terms of cost and in terms of the change in the underlying geometry."

Note this is a different phenomenon from a weak network. A weak datum shows up as a small-but-nonzero eigenvalue; gauge deficiency gives you exact zeros, and no amount of extra imagery removes them. Triggs also notes the same machinery covers other cases: uncalibrated projective reconstruction carries additional gauge groups (“the arbitrary scale factors of homogeneous projective feature representations, with their 1D rescaling gauge groups”).

Three ways to fix it

Three points freezes 9 parameters to kill a 7-dimensional symmetry, so it additionally hard-constrains the shape of that triangle; COLMAP uses it as a fallback when no suitable camera pair exists.

Why this matters far more for covariance than for the estimate

The estimate barely cares — any gauge lands on the same geometry, just expressed in different coordinates. Covariance cares enormously:

Triggs et al. 2000, §9.1source
"Covariances are averages of squared perturbations and must also be based on on-gauge perturbations (they would be infinite if we permitted perturbations along the gauge orbits, as there is no limit to these — they do not change the cost at all). So covariance matrices are gauge-dependent and in fact represent ellipsoids tangent to the gauge cross-section at the cost minimum. They can look very different for different gauges." "In visual reconstruction, the differences between object-centred and camera-centred gauges are often particularly marked. In object-centred gauges, object points appear to be relatively certain while cameras appear to have large and highly correlated uncertainties."

Two practical corollaries follow, and both are easy to get wrong:

  • “The standard deviation of point 4172” is not a well-posed quantity until you say relative to what. Only gauge-invariant functions — ratios of distances, angles, relative poses — have datum-independent uncertainty.
  • A covariance routine that fails on a free network is behaving correctly. The reduced camera system S inherits the rank deficiency, so a Cholesky of S has no answer to give. Fixing the gauge is a precondition for covariance, not an optional refinement.

Triggs’s own recommendation is a useful default: do the numerics in whatever cheap gauge is convenient, and convert afterwards. §9.5, verbatim: “it does not seem worthwhile to pay a high computational cost for gauge fixing during step prediction … the main computation can be done in any convenient, low cost gauge, and the results later transformed into the desired gauge using the gauge projector PG = 1 − G(DG)−1D. It is probably easiest to use a trivial gauge for the computation.” He also observes that inner (free-network) gauges “do not distort the intrinsic covariance structure so much as most outer ones, so they tend to have better numerical conditioning and give a more representative idea of the true accuracy of the network.”

§13 How modern SfM packages solve it

The factor-graph branch: same algebra, different worldview

g2o and GTSAM come from SLAM rather than photogrammetry, and it is worth being precise about what is and is not different.

g2o is the smaller step. It is batch Gauss-Newton/LM behind a graph-shaped API, and its paper names all the same machinery: “a naive implementation using standard methods like Gauss-Newton, Levenberg-Marquardt (LM), Gauss-Seidel relaxation, or variants of gradient descent”; “efficient calculation of the Schur reduction”; CHOLMOD, CSparse, and “PCG as an iterative method using a block-Jacobi preconditioner”. Its own framing of the scope, verbatim: “Many popular problems in robotics and computer vision including various types of simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) or bundle adjustment (BA) can be phrased as least squares optimization of an error function that can be represented by a graph.”

GTSAM is the genuine philosophical departure, and states it itself: it uses “factor graphs and Bayes networks as the underlying computing paradigm rather than sparse matrices to optimize for the most probable configuration”. Instead of assembling H and factoring it, GTSAM eliminates variables from a graph. The punchline is that these are the same operation:

Dellaert & Kaess, Factor Graphs for Robot Perception, §4.5source
"In SLAM, a naive ordering strategy is to eliminate the landmarks first, and then the poses. This is often called the 'Schur-complement trick', because when the elimination is written down at the block level the corresponding linear algebra operation involves taking the Schur complement of a matrix."

So the Schur complement is one particular elimination ordering, and sparse Cholesky is variable elimination on the associated graph. What GTSAM buys by taking the graph view seriously is incrementality: iSAM2 stores the elimination result as a Bayes tree and, when a new measurement arrives, re-eliminates only the affected part and relinearises only variables whose estimates have actually moved (“incremental variable re-ordering and fluid relinearization, eliminating the need for periodic batch steps”). Batch BA, by contrast, relinearises everything and refactors from scratch every iteration.

When each is right. Offline aerial or photogrammetric blocks are solved once, to the tightest achievable accuracy, with all data present — batch BA with a direct sparse solver is exactly right. Online SLAM adds measurements continuously and must respond in milliseconds — incremental factor-graph inference is exactly right. They are not competing answers to one question.

The lineage, and where it is going

Ⅰ

Photogrammetry, 1958–2000

Brown’s reduced normal equations (1958) are the Schur complement trick, decades before computer vision named it. Triggs et al. (2000) consolidate the field’s vocabulary and remain the reference for robustification and gauge.

Ⅱ

Internet-scale SfM, 2006–2011

Bundler brings BA to unordered photo collections on top of sba’s sparse LM. “Bundle Adjustment in the Large” (2010) shows the Schur trick is best understood as a preconditioner and that truncated-Newton with cheap preconditioners wins at scale; Ceres is its byproduct. Multicore BA (2011) moves the same PCG onto GPUs in single precision.

Ⅲ

Consolidation, 2012–2016

Visibility-based preconditioning (Kushal & Agarwal 2012) exploits scene structure for 3–5× gains. COLMAP (2016) becomes the de facto pipeline, with Ceres underneath and BA scheduled incrementally — local BA after each registration, global BA after the model grows by a set percentage.

Ⅳ

Faster solvers for the same objective, 2021–

Power Bundle Adjustment (Weber et al., CVPR 2023) expands S−1 as a truncated power series — usable as solver, preconditioner or initialiser, and now shipped inside Ceres. MegBA (ECCV 2022) distributes PCG and Schur elimination across GPUs. COLMAP 4.1.0 added Caspar, a native GPU BA backend reported as “1–2 orders of magnitude faster” than the Ceres CUDA path on medium-to-large problems. This branch is mature and drop-in: same answer, less time.

Ⅴ

Learned objectives, 2019– — a different thing entirely

BA-Net (Tang & Tan, ICLR 2019) replaces the reprojection residual with a feature-metric one and makes the whole LM loop differentiable so the features can be learned. Note what changed: not the solver, but the residual. For metric-accuracy-critical work this is active research, not a replacement for the classical pipeline — the objective no longer has a direct interpretation in pixels.

Stated as inference, not fact: Caspar’s exposed options (diag_init, diag_scaling_up/down, pcg_iter_max, pcg_rel_error_exit…) read unmistakably as an LM damping schedule wrapped around a preconditioned-CG inner solver — i.e. the same architecture as Multicore BA, on modern hardware. That is an inference from the option names, not from Caspar’s source or a paper.
